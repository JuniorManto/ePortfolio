PPG Heart Rate Sensor — ENGR 352
4-stage analog circuit · breadboard build · LTSPICE simulation
▼
S — Situation

The Challenge: Detecting a Heartbeat with Analog Circuits

Every time the heart beats, it pushes a pulse of blood through the arteries, and that pulse subtly changes how much infrared light the skin absorbs. If you shine an IR emitter against a finger and measure the reflected intensity with a photodiode, you can actually see the heartbeat as a tiny fluctuation in the detected current. This technique is called photoplethysmography, or PPG, and it is the same principle behind every modern fitness tracker and pulse oximeter.

In ENGR 352, our task was to build a PPG heart rate sensor from scratch using discrete analog components with no microcontrollers or digital shortcuts. The photodiode current sits in the range of nanoamps, and getting from that near-invisible signal to a clean, reliable LED flash for every heartbeat required four carefully engineered analog stages, each building directly on the last.

T — Task

What We Were Asked to Build

The project spanned four successive lab sessions, each delivering a working circuit block that fed directly into the next. The final design had to operate from a single 5 V USB supply, detect a real human pulse reliably, and produce an LED flash of appropriate duration for each detected heartbeat, all using only component values available in the lab inventory. My partner and I were responsible for the complete design workflow at every stage, covering theoretical analysis, LTSPICE simulation, physical breadboard implementation, and oscilloscope verification.

A — Action

The Build, Stage by Stage

Stage 1 — Lab 2
Transimpedance Amplifier

The photodiode produces a DC current of roughly 1 µA with an AC heartbeat signal of only 25 to 100 nA riding on top. We designed a transimpedance amplifier that converts this current directly into a voltage using an op-amp with a megaohm-range feedback resistor. A small feedback capacitor was added to roll off high-frequency noise and prevent oscillation. We also hand-soldered the optical sensor assembly, carefully positioning the photodiode flush against the IR emitter with a heat-shrink shroud to minimize direct IR leakage.

Stage 2 — Lab 3
Bandpass Filter and Gain Stage

Stage 1 produced a signal dominated by a large DC offset, which was not useful for triggering. Stage 2 stripped that DC component away with an AC-coupled non-inverting op-amp stage and amplified the remaining AC heartbeat signal to approximately 1 V peak. A resistor divider set the common-mode input to 2.5 V and the AC gain was tuned experimentally based on the actual signal from the sensor, completing a bandpass response across both cascaded stages.

Stage 3 — Lab 4
Hysteretic Comparator

The smooth analog heartbeat waveform needed to be converted into a clean digital edge for the monostable to trigger on. We designed a hysteretic comparator with positive feedback to create two trip points around a 2.5 V reference, so the output only switched when the input clearly crossed the defined band. This eliminated noise-induced false triggers and produced a clean 0 to 5 V square wave that fired exactly once per heartbeat.

Stage 4 — Lab 5
Monostable Multivibrator and LED Driver

A differentiator followed by an isolation diode extracted only the positive-going rising edges so the monostable fired exactly once per heartbeat. The monostable used two NPN BJTs in a cross-coupled configuration, holding the output high for a duration Ton set by the RC time constant. We calculated Ton = 167.3 ms analytically, targeting roughly 20% duty cycle at 72 BPM. Simulation confirmed 166 ms and the physical measurement came in at 170 ms, within 2% of the theoretical prediction. A third transistor drove the green LED through a current-limiting resistor, producing a visible flash synchronized to each heartbeat.

R — Result

What We Built and What It Proved

The final integrated circuit successfully detected a real human pulse and produced a consistent LED flash for every heartbeat, with a measured Ton within 2% of the theoretical prediction. No component adjustments were required after assembly and the design worked on the first build. The project demonstrated the value of validating each block independently before cascading them together. When the final system was assembled, it worked immediately.

S — Situation

The Problem: Amplifying Without Adding Noise

In any wireless receiver, whether it is a phone, a radar system, or a satellite link, the first component the antenna connects to is a low noise amplifier. The LNA's job is deceptively simple: amplify the incoming signal before it reaches the rest of the receiver chain. The problem is that every electronic component adds noise to the signal it handles, and if the first amplifier adds too much noise, no amount of downstream processing can recover the original signal quality.

This project challenged me to design a 5 GHz LNA from scratch using AWR Microwave Office and a generic BiCMOS foundry process with heterojunction bipolar transistors. The design had to simultaneously meet four demanding targets over a 200 MHz bandwidth: noise figure below 2 dB, gain above 20 dB, input return loss below negative 15 dB, and output return loss below negative 10 dB, all from a single 1.8 V supply.

T — Task

What I Was Asked to Design

Labs 3 and 4 were a two-part individual design project, each building directly on the last. Lab 3 focused on the common-emitter topology, covering bias optimization, device scaling, emitter degeneration, and input matching to produce a well-matched CE amplifier. Lab 4 then upgraded that design by adding a cascode stage and an emitter follower to push gain past the 20 dB threshold while keeping noise figure within specification. The entire workflow was simulation-based using AWR Microwave Office with no physical hardware involved.

A — Action

The Design Journey

Step 1 — Bias Optimization
Finding the Minimum Noise Figure Bias Point

Starting with a single HBT transistor and no matching networks, I swept the base bias current from 0 to 0.15 mA and plotted noise figure at 5 GHz. The result revealed a clear minimum at Ibias around 0.0075 mA with NF of approximately 7.84 dB, which was far too noisy on its own and pointed directly to the need for device scaling.

Step 2 — Device Scaling
Scaling to 24 Fingers to Align Zopt with 50 Ω

A single-finger transistor biased for minimum noise has an optimum source impedance far from 50 Ω, meaning a standard source cannot simultaneously drive the transistor at its noise minimum. I paralelled two devices at N=12 each for 24 total fingers. This scaling shifted the optimum noise impedance toward 50 Ω and dramatically dropped the minimum noise figure from 7.84 dB down to approximately 1.61 to 1.68 dB, all from choosing the right device size.

Step 3 — Emitter Degeneration and Input Matching
Adding LE and LB to Achieve 50 Ω Input Impedance

Even after scaling, Re{Zin} was only about 10 Ω. A small emitter degeneration inductor LE was added to pull Re{Zin} to 50 Ω, calculated analytically as LE = RS/ωT using fT read from the transistor Bode plot at 15.1 GHz, giving LE around 0.549 nH after tuning. The cost was visible: voltage gain dropped from 5.98 V/V to 2.51 V/V. A series base inductor LB of 0.161 nH then cancelled the remaining input reactance, pushing S11 to negative 59 dB and completing the CE amplifier with an excellent input match but only 8.67 dB of gain.

Step 4 — Cascode and Emitter Follower
Adding Q2 and Q3 to Push Gain to 21 dB

A cascode transistor Q2 was inserted between the CE output and the load to eliminate the Miller effect and push the dominant gain-limiting pole to a higher frequency. A resonant tank circuit with LT = 1 nH and a tuned capacitor CT of 0.52 pF, with series resistance to model Q=10, presented an equivalent parallel resistance of approximately 314 Ω to the collector, providing the high-impedance load needed for voltage gain. An emitter follower Q3 was added as an output buffer to drive the 50 Ω load. After iterating all tuning parameters, the cascode LNA met every specification: 21.07 dB gain, 1.78 dB noise figure, and better than negative 28 dB input return loss across the full 200 MHz bandwidth.

R — Result

From 7.84 dB Noise to a Fully Matched 21 dB Amplifier

Starting from a single transistor with a noise figure of 7.84 dB and no impedance matching, the final cascode LNA achieved 21.07 dB gain, 1.78 dB noise figure, and better than negative 28 dB input return loss across a 200 MHz bandwidth at 5 GHz. Every specification was met. The most important lesson was that noise, gain, and matching are fundamentally coupled at RF frequencies, and every design step involved a documented trade-off rather than just a parameter adjustment.

Slot Antenna Design and Simulation — ENGR 473/574
2.4 GHz antenna · AWR EM simulation · ground plane parametric study · radiation pattern analysis
▼
S — Situation

What a Slot Antenna Is and Why It Is Worth Designing

A slot antenna is one of the most elegant structures in RF engineering. Rather than adding metal to radiate, you remove it. A narrow rectangular aperture cut into a conducting ground plane is driven by a voltage source across the slot, and the resulting edge currents cause the opening itself to radiate, producing a pattern that is mathematically the complement of a dipole's. They appear in radar systems, broadcast towers, aircraft skins, and increasingly in PCB ground planes for Wi-Fi, GPS, and 5G front-ends.

In ENGR 473, the project was a parametric study: build a working simulation in AWR Microwave Office's EM solver, then systematically vary the ground plane geometry to observe and explain what happens to the radiation pattern.

T — Task

Simulate, Vary, and Interpret

The assignment required me to construct a simulation-based study of a half-wavelength slot antenna resonating at 2.4 GHz, varying the ground plane width and length independently and analyzing the resulting 3D radiation patterns, polar plots, and input impedance curves for each configuration.

A — Action

The Simulation Journey, Configuration by Configuration

Stage 1 — Initial Setup
Building the Baseline Configuration

The starting point was a finite ground plane sized at 0.8λ by 0.8λ with the slot centred at resonant dimensions of 0.447λ long by 0.1λ wide. Several early layout attempts failed to produce clean patterns due to incorrect boundary settings and oversized or undersized ground planes.

Stage 2 — Square Ground Plane Study
Comparing 0.8λ × 0.8λ Against 3λ × 3λ

The 0.8λ square ground plane produced the closest result to a theoretical donut pattern with a maximum directivity of approximately 5.74 dBi. When scaled up to 3λ by 3λ, the pattern broke down into a multi-lobe structure, confirming that a very large ground plane introduces edge diffraction effects.

Stage 3 — Width and Length Variation
Converging on 0.48λ × 0.6λ for the Cleanest Pattern

The final converged design used a ground plane of 0.48λ by 0.6λ with the slot at 0.447λ by 0.1λ, producing the cleanest toroidal radiation pattern with confirmed self-resonance at 2.397 GHz and Re(Z) peaking at approximately 418 Ω, consistent with Babinet's principle.

The Problem: Teaching a Computer to Sort by Shape

Imagine a conveyor belt moving Lego pieces past a camera, and a sorting arm that needs to route each piece to the correct bin. The raw pixel approach collapsed on realistic data, dropping to roughly 54% accuracy. The task became one of feature engineering: how do you extract information from an image that describes the shape of an object regardless of where it sits or which way it is facing?

A — Action

From Pixels to Geometry

Stage 1 — Raw Pixel Baseline
100% training accuracy on clean data, 54% on real data

Logistic regression on raw 64×64 grayscale pixels worked perfectly on a clean, aligned dataset. When applied to realistic rotated and off-centre images, it collapsed immediately — confirming that pixel templates are fragile.

Stage 2 — Feature Engineering
17-Feature Pipeline Using Hu Invariant Moments

We abandoned pixel values entirely and extracted 17 geometry-based features: area, perimeter, aspect ratio, circularity, seven Hu invariant moments, and intensity statistics — all rotation and translation invariant. Otsu thresholding produced a clean binary mask for each piece before feature extraction.

S — Situation

The Challenge: Build a Real Motor From Scratch

The ENGR 320 design project asked groups to design and build a working electric motor within a roughly $100 budget that could sustain rotation for at least 20 seconds. Our group of six decided to build a fully custom outrunner brushless DC motor with a 3D-printed structure, wound 3-phase stator, permanent magnet rotor, and a custom PCB motor controller with field-oriented control.

9Stator Coils
24Rotor Poles
3Phase Wye Config

A — Action

From CAD Model to Spinning Motor

Stage 1 — Motor Design
CAD, Pole Configuration, and Magnetic Circuit Choices

The outrunner topology was chosen for its higher torque-to-weight ratio. A 9-coil to 24-pole ratio was chosen deliberately to minimize cogging torque. All structural parts were 3D printed in PLA with mild steel rod segments pressed into the stator slots to concentrate magnetic flux.

Stage 2 — Winding and Assembly
Hand-Winding 9 Coils and Closing the Stator

Each of the 9 stator coils was wound by hand using enamelled copper wire. Getting consistent turn counts across all coils was labour-intensive, since uneven coil resistance degrades phase balance and increases vibration.

Stage 3 — Custom Controller and Testing
Sensorless FOC PCB and Live Demo

A custom double-sided PCB integrating an STM32 microcontroller was designed in KiCad and programmed with sensorless field-oriented control. The motor ran cleanly well beyond the 20-second minimum at the live class demonstration.
