PPG Heart Rate Sensor — ENGR 352
4-stage analog circuit · breadboard build · LTSPICE simulation
▼
S — Situation

The Challenge: Detecting a Heartbeat with Analog Circuits

Every time the heart beats, it pushes a pulse of blood through the arteries, and that pulse subtly changes how much infrared light the skin absorbs. If you shine an IR emitter against a finger and measure the reflected intensity with a photodiode, you can actually see the heartbeat as a tiny fluctuation in the detected current. This technique is called photoplethysmography, or PPG, and it is the same principle behind every modern fitness tracker and pulse oximeter.

In ENGR 352, our task was to build a PPG heart rate sensor from scratch using discrete analog components with no microcontrollers or digital shortcuts. The photodiode current sits in the range of nanoamps, and getting from that near-invisible signal to a clean, reliable LED flash for every heartbeat required four carefully engineered analog stages, each building directly on the last.

T — Task

What We Were Asked to Build

The project spanned four successive lab sessions, each delivering a working circuit block that fed directly into the next. The final design had to operate from a single 5 V USB supply, detect a real human pulse reliably, and produce an LED flash of appropriate duration for each detected heartbeat, all using only component values available in the lab inventory. My partner and I were responsible for the complete design workflow at every stage, covering theoretical analysis, LTSPICE simulation, physical breadboard implementation, and oscilloscope verification.

A — Action

The Build, Stage by Stage

Stage 1 — Lab 2
Transimpedance Amplifier

The photodiode produces a DC current of roughly 1 µA with an AC heartbeat signal of only 25 to 100 nA riding on top. We designed a transimpedance amplifier that converts this current directly into a voltage using an op-amp with a megaohm-range feedback resistor. A small feedback capacitor was added to roll off high-frequency noise and prevent oscillation. We also hand-soldered the optical sensor assembly, carefully positioning the photodiode flush against the IR emitter with a heat-shrink shroud to minimize direct IR leakage.

Stage 2 — Lab 3
Bandpass Filter and Gain Stage

Stage 1 produced a signal dominated by a large DC offset, which was not useful for triggering. Stage 2 stripped that DC component away with an AC-coupled non-inverting op-amp stage and amplified the remaining AC heartbeat signal to approximately 1 V peak. A resistor divider set the common-mode input to 2.5 V and the AC gain was tuned experimentally based on the actual signal from the sensor, completing a bandpass response across both cascaded stages.

Stage 3 — Lab 4
Hysteretic Comparator

The smooth analog heartbeat waveform needed to be converted into a clean digital edge for the monostable to trigger on. We designed a hysteretic comparator with positive feedback to create two trip points around a 2.5 V reference, so the output only switched when the input clearly crossed the defined band. This eliminated noise-induced false triggers and produced a clean 0 to 5 V square wave that fired exactly once per heartbeat.

Stage 4 — Lab 5
Monostable Multivibrator and LED Driver

A differentiator followed by an isolation diode extracted only the positive-going rising edges so the monostable fired exactly once per heartbeat. The monostable used two NPN BJTs in a cross-coupled configuration, holding the output high for a duration Ton set by the RC time constant. We calculated Ton = 167.3 ms analytically, targeting roughly 20% duty cycle at 72 BPM. Simulation confirmed 166 ms and the physical measurement came in at 170 ms, within 2% of the theoretical prediction. A third transistor drove the green LED through a current-limiting resistor, producing a visible flash synchronized to each heartbeat.

The final integrated circuit successfully detected a real human pulse and produced a consistent LED flash for every heartbeat, with a measured Ton within 2% of the theoretical prediction. No component adjustments were required after assembly and the design worked on the first build. The project demonstrated the value of validating each block independently before cascading them together. When the final system was assembled, it worked immediately.

S — Situation

The Problem: Amplifying Without Adding Noise

In any wireless receiver, whether it is a phone, a radar system, or a satellite link, the first component the antenna connects to is a low noise amplifier. The LNA's job is deceptively simple: amplify the incoming signal before it reaches the rest of the receiver chain. The problem is that every electronic component adds noise to the signal it handles, and if the first amplifier adds too much noise, no amount of downstream processing can recover the original signal quality.

This project challenged me to design a 5 GHz LNA from scratch using AWR Microwave Office and a generic BiCMOS foundry process with heterojunction bipolar transistors. The design had to simultaneously meet four demanding targets over a 200 MHz bandwidth: noise figure below 2 dB, gain above 20 dB, input return loss below negative 15 dB, and output return loss below negative 10 dB, all from a single 1.8 V supply.

T — Task

What I Was Asked to Design

Labs 3 and 4 were a two-part individual design project, each building directly on the last. Lab 3 focused on the common-emitter topology, covering bias optimization, device scaling, emitter degeneration, and input matching to produce a well-matched CE amplifier. Lab 4 then upgraded that design by adding a cascode stage and an emitter follower to push gain past the 20 dB threshold while keeping noise figure within specification. The entire workflow was simulation-based using AWR Microwave Office with no physical hardware involved.

A — Action

The Design Journey

Step 1 — Bias Optimization
Finding the Minimum Noise Figure Bias Point

Starting with a single HBT transistor and no matching networks, I swept the base bias current from 0 to 0.15 mA and plotted noise figure at 5 GHz. The result revealed a clear minimum at Ibias around 0.0075 mA with NF of approximately 7.84 dB, which was far too noisy on its own and pointed directly to the need for device scaling.

Step 2 — Device Scaling
Scaling to 24 Fingers to Align Zopt with 50 Ω

A single-finger transistor biased for minimum noise has an optimum source impedance far from 50 Ω, meaning a standard source cannot simultaneously drive the transistor at its noise minimum. I paralelled two devices at N=12 each for 24 total fingers. This scaling shifted the optimum noise impedance toward 50 Ω and dramatically dropped the minimum noise figure from 7.84 dB down to approximately 1.61 to 1.68 dB, all from choosing the right device size.

Step 3 — Emitter Degeneration and Input Matching
Adding LE and LB to Achieve 50 Ω Input Impedance

Even after scaling, Re{Zin} was only about 10 Ω. A small emitter degeneration inductor LE was added to pull Re{Zin} to 50 Ω, calculated analytically as LE = RS/ωT using fT read from the transistor Bode plot at 15.1 GHz, giving LE around 0.549 nH after tuning. The cost was visible: voltage gain dropped from 5.98 V/V to 2.51 V/V. A series base inductor LB of 0.161 nH then cancelled the remaining input reactance, pushing S11 to negative 59 dB and completing the CE amplifier with an excellent input match but only 8.67 dB of gain.

Step 4 — Cascode and Emitter Follower
Adding Q2 and Q3 to Push Gain to 21 dB

A cascode transistor Q2 was inserted between the CE output and the load to eliminate the Miller effect and push the dominant gain-limiting pole to a higher frequency. A resonant tank circuit with LT = 1 nH and a tuned capacitor CT of 0.52 pF, with series resistance to model Q=10, presented an equivalent parallel resistance of approximately 314 Ω to the collector, providing the high-impedance load needed for voltage gain. An emitter follower Q3 was added as an output buffer to drive the 50 Ω load. After iterating all tuning parameters, the cascode LNA met every specification: 21.07 dB gain, 1.78 dB noise figure, and better than negative 28 dB input return loss across the full 200 MHz bandwidth.

Starting from a single transistor with a noise figure of 7.84 dB and no impedance matching, the final cascode LNA achieved 21.07 dB gain, 1.78 dB noise figure, and better than negative 28 dB input return loss across a 200 MHz bandwidth at 5 GHz. Every specification was met. The most important lesson was that noise, gain, and matching are fundamentally coupled at RF frequencies, and every design step involved a documented trade-off rather than just a parameter adjustment.

Slot Antenna Design and Simulation — ENGR 473/574
2.4 GHz antenna · AWR EM simulation · ground plane parametric study · radiation pattern analysis
▼
S — Situation

What a Slot Antenna Is and Why It Is Worth Designing

A slot antenna is one of the most elegant structures in RF engineering. Rather than adding metal to radiate, you remove it. A narrow rectangular aperture cut into a conducting ground plane is driven by a voltage source across the slot, and the resulting edge currents cause the opening itself to radiate, producing a pattern that is mathematically the complement of a dipole's. The electric and magnetic fields are swapped compared to a dipole, but the overall radiation envelope is nearly identical, making slot antennas highly attractive wherever a flush, conformal, or structurally integrated antenna is needed. They appear in radar systems, broadcast towers, aircraft skins, and increasingly in PCB ground planes for Wi-Fi, GPS, and 5G front-ends.

In ENGR 473, the project was not simply to design a slot antenna to a specification. It was a parametric study: build a working simulation in AWR Microwave Office's EM solver, then systematically vary the ground plane geometry to observe and explain what happens to the radiation pattern. The underlying question was a foundational one in antenna engineering. How much does the physical boundary around an aperture actually matter for how that aperture radiates?

T — Task

Simulate, Vary, and Interpret

The assignment required me to construct a simulation-based study of a half-wavelength slot antenna resonating at 2.4 GHz inside AWR's EM environment. The target resonant frequency set the slot length at approximately 0.447λ, which corresponds to roughly 55.8 mm at 2.4 GHz with a slight shortening factor applied to achieve self-resonance. Beyond getting the antenna to resonate, the core task was to run a structured series of simulations that varied the ground plane width and length independently, and to analyze the resulting 3D radiation patterns, polar plots in the XZ and YZ planes, and input impedance curves for each configuration. The goal was not just to find a good design but to understand what controls the pattern and what trade-offs arise when the ground plane cannot be made arbitrarily large.

A — Action

The Simulation Journey, Configuration by Configuration

Stage 1 — Initial Setup
Building the Baseline Configuration

The starting point was a finite ground plane sized at 0.8λ by 0.8λ with the slot centred at resonant dimensions of 0.447λ long by 0.1λ wide. The stackup used two air layers with approximate open boundary conditions above and below the ground plane to simulate free-space radiation. Patch-style excitation ports were placed at either end of the slot to drive the voltage across the aperture. Several early layout attempts, visible in the simulation files as the "initial attempts" and "honorable mentions" configurations, failed to produce clean patterns due to incorrect boundary settings and oversized or undersized ground planes that distorted the near-field and produced asymmetric results.

Stage 2 — Square Ground Plane Study
Comparing 0.8λ × 0.8λ Against 3λ × 3λ

The 0.8λ square ground plane produced the closest result to a theoretical donut pattern, with a clean omnidirectional response in the XZ plane and a standard figure-eight in the YZ plane. The 3D directivity plot showed a smooth oblate shape with a maximum directivity of approximately 5.74 dBi. When the ground plane was scaled up dramatically to 3λ by 3λ, the pattern broke down entirely into a multi-lobe structure with several distinct grating lobes radiating in unexpected directions. The 3D pattern became a complex multi-petal shape resembling a cactus rather than a donut, confirming that a very large ground plane introduces edge diffraction effects that compete with the intended aperture radiation.

Stage 3 — Width Variation
Reducing Ground Plane Width from 0.8λ to 0.11λ

Holding the ground plane length at 0.8λ while reducing the width to 0.3λ and then further to 0.11λ revealed a consistent trend. Narrowing the width caused the two main lobes in the YZ plane to compress toward broadside and the XZ-plane omnidirectional ring to sharpen into a figure-eight pattern. At 0.11λ width the antenna began to behave more like a narrow strip radiator than a slot in a ground plane, producing a dipole-like bidirectional beam. The transition was gradual but monotonic, confirming that ground plane width directly controls the cross-polarization balance and the degree of omnidirectionality in the azimuthal plane.

Stage 4 — Length Variation and Final Design
Converging on 0.48λ × 0.6λ for the Cleanest Donut

Varying the ground plane length while holding the width at 0.8λ showed that shorter lengths pushed the pattern toward a single dominant lobe while longer lengths reintroduced multi-lobe asymmetry. The final converged design used a ground plane of 0.48λ by 0.6λ with the slot at 0.447λ by 0.1λ. This configuration produced the cleanest approximation of the desired toroidal radiation pattern: a nearly omnidirectional ring in the XZ plane, a classic figure-eight in the YZ plane, and a smooth 3D directivity envelope with no significant sidelobes. The input impedance plot confirmed self-resonance at 2.397 GHz with the imaginary part of Z(1,1) crossing zero cleanly, while the real part peaked at approximately 418 Ω, consistent with the theoretical slot impedance prediction from Babinet's principle.

R — Result

What the Study Demonstrated and What It Could Not Resolve

The parametric study confirmed that ground plane geometry is not a passive backdrop for a slot antenna. It is an active part of the radiating structure. A square ground plane near 0.8λ produces a pattern close to the theoretical toroidal ideal, but both expanding and shrinking the ground plane in specific directions predictably deforms that pattern in ways that can be understood and explained using diffraction and boundary current arguments. The final design at 0.48λ by 0.6λ achieved the cleanest omnidirectional XZ response of all configurations tested, with a smooth 3D envelope and confirmed resonance at 2.397 GHz.

There were honest limitations. The simulation used an idealized open-boundary approximation rather than a full absorbing boundary condition, which introduces some numerical reflection at the edges of the simulation domain. The port excitation model also approximated a real feed line rather than simulating a coaxial or microstrip feed directly. These simplifications are standard in educational EM simulations but would need to be addressed before the design could transition to physical fabrication and measurement.

S — Situation

The Problem: Teaching a Computer to Sort by Shape

Imagine a conveyor belt moving Lego pieces past a camera, and a sorting arm that needs to route each piece to the correct bin. The camera captures an image, and a machine learning algorithm has less than a second to decide which of four piece types it is looking at: a 2x4 rectangular brick, a 2x2 square plate, a 2x2 circular stud, or a small 2x1 rectangle. This was the real-world framing of the ENGR 418 project, and it turned out to be a considerably harder problem than it first appeared.

The difficulty was not in building a classifier. Logistic regression on raw pixels worked perfectly on a clean, centred, ideally-lit dataset where every piece sat in the same position and orientation. The challenge arrived in Stage 2, when the company revealed that real conveyor belt images include rotated pieces, off-centre framing, variable lighting, and pale pieces that barely contrast against a white background. The raw pixel approach collapsed immediately, dropping to roughly 54% accuracy on the realistic dataset, barely better than guessing. The task became one of feature engineering: how do you extract information from an image that describes the shape of an object regardless of where it sits or which way it is facing?

T — Task

Build a Rotation-Invariant, Position-Independent Classifier

Stage 1 required a linear 4-class logistic regression classifier trained directly on raw grayscale pixel values from images resized to at most 64x64 pixels. I was required to test performance across a range of input sizes and report accuracy and confusion matrices for both training and testing sets. Stage 2 then raised the stakes: apply that same stage 1 pipeline to a new, harder dataset to confirm it fails, and then replace raw pixels with a compact engineered feature vector of at most 40 features per image that could handle rotation, translation, and lighting variation. Both stages were implemented in Python using a group of two, with a formal written report required for Stage 2.

A — Action

From Pixels to Geometry, Stage by Stage

Stage 1 — Raw Pixel Baseline
Establishing What a Simple Approach Can and Cannot Do

The Stage 1 pipeline was intentionally constrained. Each RGB image was converted to grayscale and resized to a target resolution, then the pixel values were flattened into a feature vector and fed directly into a multinomial logistic regression classifier. Testing across input sizes from 8x8 up to 64x64 pixels showed that on the clean, aligned dataset the model achieved 100% training accuracy. The patterns were so consistent that pixel values alone were sufficient when position and orientation never varied. This result was informative in its own way: it told us that raw pixels can learn appearance templates, but templates are fragile.

Stage 2 — Feature Engineering Design
Building a 17-Feature Pipeline Around Geometry and Invariant Moments

When the Stage 1 pipeline was applied to the harder Stage 2 dataset with rotated and off-centre pieces, test accuracy dropped to 53.70% at best. The root cause was clear: small rotations and translations completely rearranged which pixels had which values, so the classifier had no stable reference to learn from. Our response was to abandon pixel values entirely and instead extract features that describe the shape of the piece rather than its appearance in any given position. The preprocessing pipeline began with brightness and contrast enhancement to handle pale pieces on white backgrounds, then converted to grayscale, applied a slight blur to suppress noise, and finally used Otsu's thresholding method to produce a clean binary mask isolating the Lego piece as a white region on a black field. From the largest connected contour in that mask, we extracted 17 features: area, perimeter, bounding box width and height, aspect ratio, the ratio of contour area to bounding box area, a circularity metric, seven Hu invariant moments, and the mean and standard deviation of grayscale intensity inside the mask. All features were standardized before training.

Stage 3 — Validation and Analysis
Understanding Where the Model Succeeds and Where It Still Struggles

The Stage 2 pipeline achieved 95.37% training accuracy and 87.04% test accuracy on the 108-image test set. Rectangular 2x4 bricks were classified perfectly at 27/27. The remaining errors concentrated in two failure modes. The first was segmentation failure on very pale pieces, where a white or light-coloured brick against a white background did not produce a strongly bimodal grayscale histogram, causing Otsu's threshold to either miss portions of the piece or include background. When the contour was wrong, all downstream features derived from it became unreliable. The second failure mode was intrinsic class similarity, where a slightly eroded circular piece began to resemble a square in its geometric moments, and a poorly segmented 2x4 could produce measurements closer to a 2x1 in aspect ratio. Both failure modes were documented honestly in the Stage 2 report alongside the confusion matrices for both stages.

R — Result

A 33-Percentage-Point Improvement and an Honest Accounting of What Remains

Moving from raw pixel logistic regression to a 17-feature engineered pipeline improved test accuracy from 53.70% to 87.04% on the same realistic dataset, a gain of more than 33 percentage points using the same underlying classifier. The smaller gap between training accuracy (95.37%) and test accuracy (87.04%) compared to the Stage 1 model's gap indicated better generalization rather than memorization of training images. Hu invariant moments were the most important contribution, providing rotation-invariant shape descriptors that allowed the model to treat a 2x4 brick at any angle as the same class.

The remaining 13% test error had a clear root cause: the Otsu segmentation pipeline depends on there being a meaningful intensity gap between the piece and the background. When that gap is absent, as it is for pale or white pieces under bright lighting, the features degrade and so does the classification. This is a limitation of the chosen segmentation strategy and not a fundamental limitation of the feature engineering approach. More robust segmentation using edge-based or deep learning methods would likely push accuracy higher, and that path was identified as the natural next step in the project report.

S — Situation

The Challenge: Build a Real Motor From Scratch

The ENGR 320 design project asked groups to design and build a working electric motor or generator from household and electronic components, within a roughly $100 budget, that could sustain rotation for at least 20 seconds. The grading rewarded design sophistication, application of magnetic circuit and motor theory, and depth of engineering beyond the simple wire-and-magnet Beakman motors found in online tutorials. The project brief explicitly noted that brushless DC motors typically receive the highest grades, since they demonstrate a more complete understanding of electromagnetic principles.

Our group of six decided from the beginning that we were not going to build something simple. We designed a fully custom outrunner brushless DC motor with a 3D-printed structure, a wound 3-phase stator, a permanent magnet rotor, and a custom-designed PCB motor controller programmed with field-oriented control. The result was one of the most technically ambitious projects in the course.

T — Task

Design Every Layer of a Brushless Motor System

The project required our group to design and build both the motor and its control system entirely from scratch, with no existing motor parts permitted. This meant designing the rotor and stator geometry in CAD, 3D printing the structural components in PLA, sourcing and winding the stator coils by hand, press-fitting the mild steel core segments, and integrating the neodymium permanent magnets into the rotor. It also meant designing and fabricating a custom double-sided PCB motor controller in KiCad, programming an STM32 microcontroller with sensorless field-oriented control, and validating the full electromechanical system in the final weeks of the course. Every member had a defined role, but every member also had to understand how the entire system worked well enough to answer technical questions during the live demo.

A — Action

From CAD Model to Spinning Motor, Layer by Layer

Stage 1 — Motor Design
CAD, Pole Configuration, and Magnetic Circuit Choices

The motor topology was chosen as an outrunner, where the rotor surrounds the stator and spins on the outside. This configuration produces a higher torque-to-weight ratio than an inrunner because the permanent magnets act at a larger radius from the shaft, increasing the moment arm for each magnetic force interaction. The rotor was designed with 24 alternating-polarity neodymium magnet poles and the stator with 9 wound coils arranged in a 3-phase wye configuration with 3 coils per phase. This 9-coil to 24-pole ratio was chosen deliberately to minimize cogging torque and produce smoother rotation at low speeds. All structural parts, including the rotor shell, stator frame, and mounting base, were modelled in CAD and 3D printed in PLA, with mild steel rod segments pressed into the stator slots to concentrate the magnetic flux through each coil.

Stage 2 — Winding and Assembly
Hand-Winding 9 Coils and Closing the Stator Without Air Gaps

Each of the 9 stator coils was wound by hand using enamelled copper wire around the mild steel core segments. Getting consistent turn counts and tight, evenly-distributed winding across all coils was one of the most labour-intensive parts of the project, since uneven coil resistance directly degrades phase balance and increases vibration. Closing the stator assembly presented its own challenge: the rotor shell needed to seat concentrically around the stator with a controlled and uniform air gap. Too large a gap reduces the flux linkage and drops torque, while misalignment causes vibration and uneven magnetic loading. The base was also redesigned mid-build to provide better strain relief for the three phase wires and a more secure mount during high-speed operation.

Stage 3 — Custom Controller
Designing and Fabricating a Sensorless FOC PCB

Rather than using a commercial ESC, one team member designed a fully custom motor controller in KiCad. The double-sided PCB integrated an STM32 microcontroller and a dedicated motor driver IC to avoid MOSFET gate drive configuration issues. The controller was programmed using the STM32 Motor Control Software Development Kit to implement sensorless field-oriented control, which uses measured phase current rather than a physical Hall-effect sensor to infer rotor position and maintain synchronous commutation. This meant the controller could adaptively ramp up current at startup to overcome static friction and then transition to closed-loop operation once the motor was spinning. The board was manufactured in the US to meet the project timeline, though it arrived with meaningful import fees.

Stage 4 — Integration and Testing
Phase Testing, Validation, and the Live Demo

Before the custom controller was ready, the motor was validated using a commercial servo tester and ESC to confirm that the coil windings, rotor poles, and mechanical assembly were correct. Phase testing confirmed that all three wye-connected phases were producing balanced back-EMF waveforms. Once the custom PCB was ready and programmed, the full system was tested together. The motor ran cleanly with minimal vibration at low speeds and produced strong torque at the shaft, spinning the propeller attachment mounted to the rotor during the live demo. The final motor ran well in excess of the 20-second minimum requirement.

Connecting the Unconnected:
RF Communications from the Sky

A modular multi-protocol wireless communication system designed for high-altitude balloon deployment, bringing internet access to remote communities and disaster-response personnel who need it most.

S — Situation

The Problem Worth Solving

Billions of people live in areas where terrestrial internet infrastructure simply does not reach. The cost and logistics of laying cable across remote or mountainous terrain is prohibitive, and in emergency scenarios even existing infrastructure can collapse entirely. Our client, Dr. Anas Chaaban at UBC Okanagan, posed the question that framed our entire project: could a balloon hovering above the clouds do what a tower on the ground cannot?

The target use case was specific: emergency communications and low-bandwidth internet access for remote communities and disaster-response teams. But the engineering challenge was broad. We needed a system that was lightweight enough to fly, power-efficient enough to run on batteries, affordable enough for a student budget, and compliant with Canadian radio regulations.

T — Task

What We Were Asked to Build

Our team of six was tasked with designing, building, and validating a complete RF communications payload suitable for deployment on a high-altitude balloon. The system had to meet four hard constraints simultaneously: mass under 2 kg, battery runtime of at least 30 minutes, total cost under $500 CAD, and compliance with ISED RSS-247 Canadian radio regulations.

My personal role spanned prototype assembly and testing, Wi-Fi HaLow and LoRa module integration, hardware validation, and contributions to the Final Design Report. I was the primary engineer responsible for validating the HaLow and LTE subsystems and confirming the end-to-end communication chain.

A — Action

The Design Journey

Stage 1 — Architecture Selection

We compared three candidate architectures: a simple LoRa point-to-point link, an extended-range LoRa gateway, and an SDR-based LTE-style link. The SDR approach offered flexibility but came in at nearly five times the cost, drew 6W of continuous power, and was too heavy for balloon deployment. LoRa emerged as the right foundation, with sub-$210 cost, around 0.05W steady-state power draw, and exceptional receiver sensitivity for long-range links.

Stage 2 — LoRa MVP Build

Rather than designing a fixed system from the start, we committed to a modular architecture. The core platform was a Raspberry Pi Zero 2W running Linux as the host, with peripherals connected over USB and SPI. We built a minimum viable prototype centred on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller paired with an SX1262 LoRa radio transceiver, and integrated the Reticulum network stack as an abstraction layer that allowed the system to gracefully fall back to LoRa if higher-bandwidth links failed.

Stage 3 — Adding Wi-Fi HaLow

With a working LoRa baseline, we added Wi-Fi HaLow (802.11ah), a sub-1 GHz Wi-Fi variant designed for long-range low-power IoT applications. Each added layer was evaluated against the same scorecard: mass added, power consumed, range gained, and complexity introduced.

Stage 4 — LTE Backhaul Integration

A 4G/LTE modem was added to connect the entire balloon node to the public internet, completing the chain: a ground user's phone connects over HaLow to the balloon, which relays traffic via LTE to the internet. The increased power draw required a redesigned enclosure with thermal tiering to isolate heat from the Raspberry Pi and prevent thermal throttling.

Final Design — Phased Validation

Each subsystem was validated independently before integration. The LoRa baseline was field-tested across six locations in Kelowna, BC, confirming a 5.4 km link at 75% link quality and 2.2 dB SNR. HaLow was validated indoors achieving 4,929 kbps average throughput. The full end-to-end chain was confirmed with a live speed test at 0.39 Mbps download and 0.45 Mbps upload, well above the 2 kbps design floor.

R — Result

What We Achieved and What We Learned

The final system met every hard constraint: under budget, within mass limits, regulatory-compliant, and capable of end-to-end communication. The modular phased design approach proved essential. By validating each layer independently before integration, we could isolate failures, debug efficiently, and avoid the compounding issues that plagued early prototypes where everything was being tested simultaneously.

There were also honest limitations. The LoRa-to-HAP integration failed at 11.5 km outdoors, exceeding our proven 5.4 km range. HaLow and LTE were tested indoors only due to time constraints, and no high-altitude balloon deployment was completed. These limitations point directly to the next phase of the project: outdoor range characterization, RF link optimization, and eventually putting this payload on an actual balloon.

Reflection

Looking Back, Moving Forward

"Engineering is not just about solving problems. It is about understanding why the problem exists in the first place."

On the Design Process

This course taught me that engineering design is never linear, and that structure is what keeps it from becoming chaos. The most important thing I took away from this capstone was the value of iterative, evidence-based decision-making. We did not just pick a technology and build it. We compared architectures, scored them against real criteria, built a minimum viable prototype, and only then added complexity. Every failure, whether a link that would not connect at range or a module that crashed under load, taught us more than any clean success would have. The design process, I now understand, is not about getting it right the first time. It is about building a framework rigorous enough to eventually find what right means.

On What It Means to Be an Engineer

The most important attributes of an engineer, I believe, are intellectual honesty and adaptability. You have to be willing to acknowledge when your design does not work, not quietly adjust the numbers but genuinely reconsider your assumptions. On this project, our LoRa-to-HAP link failed at 11.5 km. We documented that failure honestly and explained why. That honesty is what engineering ethics actually looks like in practice. Beyond technical skill, I also learned that collaboration is not as simple as it sounds, even working with people you know and trust. Team dynamics require active investment. There were moments where assumptions about who was doing what led to real friction, and navigating those moments professionally was as much a part of being an engineer as writing the code or running the tests.

On the Engineer's Role in Society

Working on a system designed to bring internet access to underserved regions gave me a new appreciation for the social dimension of engineering. Every technical decision, which protocol to use, how much power to draw, how much it costs to build, has a downstream effect on real people. This project, alongside my earlier work redesigning a signals course from scratch, reinforced the idea that engineers do not just build things. They shape access, opportunity, and communication for entire communities. That responsibility is not abstract. It is embedded in every design choice.

On My Own Strengths and Growth

My strengths lie in breadth and curiosity. I genuinely enjoy learning across domains, from RF circuit design to machine learning to embedded systems, and cross-disciplinary thinking has consistently led me to more creative solutions. Where I need to grow is in depth, moving beyond competence in any given area toward real expertise. I also want to develop stronger instincts for the business and financial dimensions of engineering, understanding not just whether something works but whether it creates lasting value. And I want to become a better collaborator, particularly in ambiguous team situations where leadership is not formally assigned. That is the kind of growth that only comes from experience, and I intend to seek it out deliberately.
